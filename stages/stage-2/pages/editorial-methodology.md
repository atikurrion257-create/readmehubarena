# Editorial Methodology

This page explains the standards ReadMeHub intends to use when preparing and maintaining software and web hosting guides. It is an editorial policy, not evidence that a particular product has been tested. Before this policy is published, the site operator must confirm that the people, records and review process needed to follow it are in place.

## Start with the reader's decision

An evaluation begins with a specific question: for example, whether a project-management tool fits an independent worker, or which costs matter when selecting managed WordPress hosting. The question determines which features, limitations and prices are relevant. It also makes it easier to explain who may benefit from a product and who should consider something else.

A guide should identify the intended reader, the criteria used and the scope of the information available. We do not assume that a product that suits one workflow will suit every team, budget, region or level of experience. If a meaningful criterion cannot be checked, the guide should say so rather than quietly filling the gap.

## How products are selected

For a category guide, the proposed selection process starts with products relevant to the reader's task. A shortlist can include a product with no affiliate program, and commercial availability alone is not a reason to include or exclude a product. Where a product's terms, pricing or capabilities cannot be checked, that limitation affects what can responsibly be said about it.

A shortlist should explain its selection criteria and the reason each option is included. A comparison should use the same documented criteria for each product wherever possible. Differences in plans, team size, billing period or regional availability should be made clear so a reader can see when two figures are not directly comparable.

Selection is not a claim of a complete market survey. A guide should not suggest that every available product was evaluated unless a real, documented process supports that statement.

## Two evidence modes

### Mode A: research-based evaluation

Mode A is a research draft based on available first-party material such as a vendor's product documentation, plan descriptions and pricing pages. Its evidence stamp reads **“Research-Based Evaluation (Hands-On Testing Scheduled).”** It should identify the source and date for changeable claims and distinguish what the vendor documents from what ReadMeHub has observed.

In Mode A, hands-on testing has not yet been conducted for the evaluation. The plan tested and test date are not applicable. A Mode A page must not claim “we tested,” give a measured speed or reliability result, invent a screenshot, or assign a test-derived star rating. A best-of page is a research-based shortlist explained by documented criteria, not a ranking of supposed test results.

Mode A copy can describe what documentation says a plan includes, while acknowledging that actual performance and suitability can vary. It cannot establish how a workflow felt in practice or whether a feature behaved as expected in an account. Those questions need a separate, recorded test.

### Mode B: hands-on tested evaluation

Mode B requires a dated test record and supporting screenshot, plus an editorial check that the evidence supports the page's claims. The record should identify the product and exact plan, test date, workflows attempted, steps, relevant settings, observations, limitations, upgrade triggers and any important failures or deal-breakers. A screenshot supports the record but is not a substitute for explaining what was done.

The page should report only what was observed in the stated plan and workflow. A test of one configuration does not establish an outcome for all users or future versions. A feature not encountered or checked should not be described as observed. Claims about speed, time, reliability or comparative performance require the actual measurement, conditions and relevant context; otherwise they are omitted.

A verified test log is needed before an evaluation may be presented as hands-on tested. At the time this draft was prepared, no product test logs were supplied for this build. Commercial drafts remain blocked from publication pending the required evidence and publication review.

## How we record workflow friction

When a hands-on worksheet includes a friction score, it describes the difficulty observed in a defined workflow, not the quality of the whole product. Use a five-point scale in which **1 is least friction and 5 is most friction**:

1. **Minimal:** the documented task was completed directly without a meaningful obstacle.
2. **Low:** a small clarification or extra step was needed, but the task remained straightforward.
3. **Moderate:** the task required noticeable setup, interpretation or workarounds.
4. **High:** repeated obstacles or significant configuration made the task difficult.
5. **Very high:** a major obstacle prevented completion or required an impractical workaround in the tested conditions.

A score should be accompanied by the workflow, plan, date, steps and observations that justify it. It is not a benchmark, an aggregate user rating or a universal claim. If the worksheet does not contain enough detail to support a score, leave the score blank. Numeric product ratings and rating schema are not part of a research-only evaluation.

## Pricing, renewals and verification

Prices and plan limits are changeable, so the intended source of record is the provider's own pricing or official plan page. A pricing entry should identify the plan, currency and billing interval, the monthly price and any annual-billing equivalent, relevant limits, source URL and verification date. If a price requires a minimum number of seats, a promotion, a specific country or another condition, the reader should see that condition with the price.

Introductory and renewal prices must be separate. Where only one is available, the other should be marked unverified rather than inferred. A free tier should not be presented as unlimited if its documented limits matter to the decision. We do not treat an affiliate commission or payout as a product price or a reason to recommend a tool.

The proposed maintenance rule is to reverify changeable pricing and affiliate terms at least every **90 days**. A record older than 90 days is stale and needs re-verification; a visible date is not proof that the price is still current today. If the first-party source cannot be checked, the page must retain an explicit verification item and stay blocked from publication rather than substitute an estimate.

## How comparisons and shortlists are built

A comparison uses a matched set of criteria selected for the reader's task. Depending on the category, those criteria might include workflow fit, plan limits, total cost, collaboration, migration, support or documented restrictions. Each product should be considered on the same basis, with plan differences identified.

A best-of guide should explain the selection rubric before the recommendations. Each pick should include a fit rationale and a reason someone might skip it. Rankings in a research draft reflect documented criteria, not undocumented hands-on performance. A later hands-on update may change a conclusion only when the new evidence and its scope are recorded.

A table is an aid to comparison, not a replacement for context. A single cell cannot convey every condition, and “not verified” is preferable to a guessed value. Readers should use the linked current provider source to confirm terms that matter to their decision.

## Sources, updates and corrections

A page should link to the sources supporting material facts and show relevant verification or update dates. Source quality matters: a first-party price page can establish a listed price at a point in time, but it does not by itself establish that a product is easy to use or suitable for a particular team. Dated test notes support observations only within the tested conditions.

When a material error or stale claim is identified, the intended process is to check the report against the relevant evidence, correct or qualify the affected content, and update the page's record. If a correction cannot yet be verified, the affected claim should not remain presented as established fact. Readers can use [Contact and Report an Error](/contact/) to send a page URL, the disputed statement and a supporting source.

## Affiliate relationships and editorial separation

ReadMeHub may earn commissions from some qualifying links. The intended separation is straightforward: an affiliate relationship does not purchase placement, alter a verdict or remove a relevant tradeoff. Products without an affiliate program can still be discussed when they are relevant to the reader's question.

A commercial page should include a plain-language disclosure before its first affiliate link. The [Affiliate Disclosure](/affiliate-disclosure/) explains how commissions may arise and what they do—and do not—mean for the reader. An affiliate relationship and a product's current price or quality are separate facts; neither should be inferred from the other.

## Limits of the guidance

A guide is informational, not a guarantee that a product will suit a particular situation. Vendor interfaces, terms and availability can change after a page is checked. Readers should verify material terms with the provider, assess their own requirements and make an independent decision. For questions about this policy, see [About ReadMeHub](/about/) or use the [contact page](/contact/).
